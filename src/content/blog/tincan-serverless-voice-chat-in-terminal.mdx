When playing games or pairing on code with friends, we almost always connect to someone else's server for voice chat. If Discord goes down, if your account gets suspended, or if you simply don't want your private conversation traversing someone else's infrastructure, you don't have many options. Self-hosted solutions like Mumble exist, but someone still has to rent a VPS, configure firewalls, and forward ports.

So I started wondering: *"Can we do this with absolutely zero servers?"*

That's how **tincan** came to life.

The concept is as elementary as two tin cans and a string: whoever opens the app first creates the room, the terminal prints an invite code or generates a speakable 4-word passphrase, you pass it to your friends, and they join from anywhere in the world. No VPN, no port forwarding, no account registration, no central server. Everything lives inside the terminal.

```bash
# Open a named room (generates a 4-word passphrase):
tincan host lobby --name alice

# Or open an unnamed room behind an invite code:
tincan host --name alice --password secret
```

Joining is a single command:

```bash
tincan join lobby --name bob
# or using the 63-character raw invite code:
tincan join n73w-kuqc-uog2-... --name bob --password secret
```

## From a 63-Character Key to 4 Speakable Words

In the initial prototype, there was only one way into a room: the terminal printed the host's 63-character Base32 public key. Under the hood, tincan uses [iroh](https://iroh.computer) for peer-to-peer connectivity: you locate the target machine by its public key, QUIC encrypts the connection end-to-end, and the key itself cryptographically verifies the peer's identity.

A 63-character string is trivial to copy and paste, but try reading it over a phone call—trust me, I tried.

With v0.3.0, I introduced named rooms with derived passphrases:

```text
room:        lobby
passphrase:  chestnut-ferry-lens-moss
```

The room's address is now derived directly from `Argon2id(passphrase, room name)`. That means all your friends need are two things you can speak out loud or scribble on a whiteboard.

Naturally, there is an honest architectural trade-off: in a named room, the coordinator key identifies the *passphrase*, not a specific physical machine. Anyone who holds that passphrase can technically spawn a rival room under the same name or publish their own address records. For a close circle of friends, that risk is negligible; if you require absolute certainty, you can host without a room name and share the raw 63-character public key instead.

## Audio Does Not Pass Through the Coordinator: Star vs. Mesh

Decoupling the architecture into two distinct planes was the single best decision in the project:

1. **The control plane is a star topology:** Whoever opens the room acts as the coordinator. The participant roster, channel metadata, and text chat flow through the coordinator. This traffic footprint is negligible—barely a few hundred bytes per second.
2. **The voice plane is a full mesh:** Everyone inside the same voice channel establishes direct P2P connections to each other, streaming Opus packets as QUIC datagrams.

```text
        [alice: coordinator]
         /        |       \          ── control (reliable stream)
       bob      carol     dave
         \________|_______/          ── voice (mesh, direct datagrams)
```

If voice had been routed through the coordinator, the host's residential upload bandwidth would bottleneck the audio quality for the entire room. In a peer-to-peer mesh, a 6-person room only demands about **~160 kbps** of upload per participant.

The trade-off is scalability: in a full mesh, every peer broadcasts to every other peer. Past 8–10 people, bandwidth consumption skyrockets, requiring a Selective Forwarding Unit (SFU) to mix audio into a single stream. But for small squads gaming or collaborating, 2–6 participants is the sweet spot.

Terminal UIs often suffer from sterile, uninspired typography. With Ratatui, I wanted tincan to have genuine tactile personality.

The palette is metallic: tin brown ground, brass accents, and the two shades of green that copper naturally acquires—bright patina for live state, deeper verdigris for an established link.

The line traversing the middle of the terminal is the connection string:
- When peers connect directly via P2P hole punching, the string is **taut and continuous**.
- When traffic is forced to fall back through a relay, the string sags into a **dashed line**.
- When audio dropouts or packet loss occur, the string **visibly frays**.

My favorite touch: whenever someone speaks, a **traveling pulse** slides across that string at the exact speed of the round-trip latency (RTT). Rather than squinting at ping numbers in a corner, you literally watch network latency ripple across the wire.

```text
Shortcut Cheatsheet:
  Tab / Shift+Tab   : Cycle through channels
  F2                : Join / leave the voice of the viewed channel
  F3                : Mute / unmute your microphone
  F4                : Push-to-talk (toggle)
  F5                : Deafen (hear nobody; also mutes microphone)
  F6                : Audio settings (devices, input levels, noise floor)
  ↑ / ↓             : Select a participant in the roster
  ← / →             : Adjust that person's volume locally
  Ctrl+K            : Silence that participant (local only)
```

In the roster, real-time volume meters track each person's speech dynamics. If a friend's microphone is blowing out your ears, select their name with the arrow keys and nudge their volume down with `←`, or silence them entirely with `Ctrl+K`. Even when silenced, their meter continues to dance subtly in dim grey, ensuring you can still see that they are speaking without being blasted by their audio.

The channel you are reading and the channel you are speaking in are completely decoupled. A `>` indicator marks your active reading view, while `🔊` denotes your active voice channel. You can read `#general` while actively communicating in `#gaming`.

## Real-World Audio: Resampling and the 10 ms RNNoise Trade-Off

On a whiteboard, digital audio seems simple: read PCM samples, encode with Opus, ship packets, decode, feed the DAC.

In reality, audio hardware is messy. Initially, I took the lazy shortcut: "If your device isn't 48 kHz, I won't support it." But in the real world, users plug in 16 kHz Bluetooth headsets (like AirPods in call profile) or 96 kHz studio DACs. Today, tincan implements bidirectional **cubic interpolation resampling**; whatever sample rate your hardware exposes is smoothly interpolated to and from Opus's native 48 kHz.

The second—and far more difficult—problem was ambient noise.

A naive noise gate only checks decibel thresholds: it cuts off when you are silent, but the instant you speak, your mechanical keyboard clatter, desk fans, or street noise leak through unattenuated.

To solve this, I integrated **RNNoise** (`nnnoiseless`), a lightweight recurrent neural network trained to separate human speech from stationary noise. You can hammer on clicky Blue switches while speaking, and the listener on the other end hears pristine voice with zero keyboard chatter.

However, good engineering demands honesty about trade-offs: RNNoise relies on an overlap-add windowing algorithm. It cannot emit a processed frame until it inspects the succeeding frame. This imposes a **mandatory 10 ms latency penalty on the capture path**, regardless of whether noise is present.

Balanced against tincan's 20 ms frames and 3-frame jitter buffer (~60 ms), 10 ms is an exceptional bargain for crystal-clear voice. But if you are on a studio condenser in an isolated booth and want absolute minimum latency, hit `n` on the `F6` audio settings screen: neural suppression switches off, and that 10 ms returns straight to your budget.

## The Mute Key Was Sending Messages

My absolute favorite bug during development:

Early on, I mapped microphone mute to `Ctrl+M` and channel navigation to `Ctrl+J`. It felt intuitive. But during initial tests, every time someone tried to mute their mic, empty messages flooded the chat pane.

The reason? In terminal escape sequences, `Ctrl+M` is byte `0x0D` (Carriage Return), and `Ctrl+J` is `0x0A` (Line Feed). In other words, to the terminal emulator, both keystrokes literally *are* **Enter**. The terminal doesn't announce "the user pressed Ctrl+M"; it announces "the user pressed Enter".

That's why audio toggles moved to function keys (F2, F3, F5). Building terminal software forces you to respect the raw byte semantics of 1970s teletype protocols.

A similar constraint dictates Push-to-Talk: most terminal emulators do not emit `key-release` events. As a result, in `--ptt` mode, F4 functions as an on/off toggle rather than a hold-down key.

## Passwords and P2P Transparency

Room passphrases never travel over the network in plaintext. The coordinator issues a fresh, single-use nonce for every incoming connection; the client replies with an `Argon2id`-stretched key hashing that nonce via a `BLAKE2b` MAC. Because the challenge changes on every handshake, captured handshake payloads cannot be replayed.

Remember: the passphrase is an **admission control** gate, not the transport cipher. QUIC itself enforces end-to-end encryption.

It's also worth discussing what "serverless" actually means:
No tincan company servers exist, no analytics are logged, and no central database tracks rooms. However, for two endpoints behind symmetric NATs to discover each other, tincan relies on Number Zero's public iroh infrastructure (`dns.iroh.link` pkarr records and fallback relay nodes).

And crucially: **On a real string, the other end knows your physical location.**
Centralized platforms (like Discord) route all traffic through proxy servers, masking your IP address from other users. Because tincan connects participants directly via peer-to-peer mesh, everyone in a room can see each other's IP addresses. The freedom from corporate surveillance means accepting direct peer transparency among the people you invite.

## Installation and Getting Started

In earlier releases, compiling Opus from source required wrestling with local C build tools. In v0.3.0, installation is seamless across major platforms:

**Homebrew (macOS & Linux):**
```bash
brew tap bilalyazicioglu/tap && brew install tincan
```

**NPM / NPX (Zero-install instant run):**
```bash
npx tincan-cli host
```

**One-Line Shell Installer:**
```bash
curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/bilalyazicioglu/tincan-cli/main/install.sh | sh
```

*(Prebuilt standalone binaries are also published for Apple Silicon, Intel macOS, Linux x86/ARM64, and Windows on the GitHub releases page.)*

The entire codebase is open source: [github.com/bilalyazicioglu/tincan-cli](https://github.com/bilalyazicioglu/tincan-cli).
For deeper architectural deep-dives and interface design philosophy, take a look at the repo's [Wiki](https://github.com/bilalyazicioglu/tincan-cli/tree/main/docs/wiki).
